**Overview of the Current Situation: Substantial Erosion of Capital Efficiency Due to Grid Connection Queues**

According to the IEA's "Electricity 2026" report, as of early 2026, the total global capacity queued for grid connection exceeds 2,500 GW (including renewable energy, energy storage, and large load projects such as data centers). The US market accounts for the largest share (LBNL data shows approximately 2,290 GW), followed by Europe. In contrast, China, leveraging national-level coordinated planning and proactive UHV (Ultra High Voltage) construction, has fundamentally resolved the kind of structural congestion seen in the US and Europe, where "projects pile up and grids become paralyzed." Grid investment is proactively aligned with renewable energy expansion, maintaining a leading position in actual grid connection efficiency.

**Breakdown of Core Drivers Behind Grid Connection Cycle Differences**

Differences in grid connection speed essentially result from a combination of institutional efficiency, network redundancy, and cost-sharing mechanisms, which can be summarized into the following four interrelated dimensions:

1. Grid-Source Coordination Model

China adopts a state-led centralized planning model, with generation bases and UHV transmission lines being planned, constructed, and commissioned simultaneously, enabling the grid to proactively match generation expansion. The US and EU, on the other hand, are primarily market-driven, with developers selecting sites and submitting applications first, and the grid responding afterward, leading to project backlogs and resource mismatches.

2. Upgrade Cost-Sharing Mechanism

In China, the basic upgrade costs are distributed across society through transmission and distribution tariffs, significantly lowering the threshold for individual projects. In the US, developers are required to bear the vast majority of grid upgrade costs (for some projects, this exceeds 40%), directly raising entry barriers and triggering project cancellations. The EU and India adopt hybrid cost-sharing models, but land and cross-border coordination introduce additional uncertainties.

3. Approval and System Simulation Complexity

The volatility of renewables necessitates more sophisticated system stability simulations (SIS). In any mature global power market—be it China, Europe, or North America—simulation studies are a statutory prerequisite for obtaining grid acceptance for generator units (especially wind, PV, energy storage, and other renewables) or large loads. In the US, excessively long queues have led to simulation studies becoming trapped in a cycle of delays. China, through standardized interruptible loads and grid-forming inverter technology, locks in stability in advance, significantly improving approval efficiency.

Mainstream Global Standards and Requirements:

·      China: Compliance with national standards (e.g., GB/T 19963 for wind, GB/T 19964 for PV) is mandatory. Detailed model parameters must be submitted to the grid company prior to connection, followed by equivalent modeling and simulation verification.

·      North America (NERC/IEEE): IEEE 2800 is currently the latest international benchmark for renewable integration, requiring validated models (with very high modeling fidelity).

·      Europe (ENTSO-E): Emphasizes unit capability verification, requiring developers to provide detailed model library files, which must be certified through third-party simulations.

4. Equipment and Land Bottlenecks

Global lead times for key equipment such as large transformers have generally extended to 18-24 months. China has a clear advantage due to its complete supply chain; the US and India, however, are further hampered by labor shortages and land acquisition litigation, causing additional delays of 2-3 years.

**Grid Connection Reform Trends and Policy Developments for 2025-2026:**

Divergence is intensifying, but reforms are accelerating. Each country is showing distinctly different trends. China continues to reinforce its grid-first logic. From 2026 to 2030, State Grid plans to add 15 new UHV lines, with an average annual increase in renewable energy consumption capacity of about 200 TWh and a 35% increase in interprovincial transmission capacity; the National Energy Administration is coordinating fast-track UHV construction and top-level design for the new power system. Grid connection cycles for large base projects remain stable at under one year, curtailment rates continue to decline, and expansion certainty leads globally.

In the US, surging demand from AI data centers is expected to add 166 GW of new peak load over the next five years, becoming the biggest catalyst for reform. In 2025, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the Department of Energy jointly promoted regional transmission organizations to implement cluster studies, automatic withdrawal penalties, and fast-track channels for high-resource-adequacy projects, while also introducing co-located load rules and AI-priority access channels. The volume of signed agreements increased by 33% YoY, and grid connection times for some data centers are expected to be reduced from over five years to 2-3 years; however, traditional renewable projects are still constrained by upgrade cost-sharing and environmental litigation, and long-term waiting pressures have not been fundamentally alleviated. This structural tilt means capital will increasingly favor "policy-friendly" loads (such as data centers and energy storage).

The EU's Grid Package, officially released in December 2025, marks the transition to mandatory reform implementation. The package enforces advance investment planning, flexible access mechanisms, and a unified host capacity map, while simultaneously revising TEN-E (Trans-European Networks for Energy regulation) and permitting procedures, requiring member states to implement advance investment incentives and public participation mechanisms. REPowerEU (Europe's affordable, secure, and sustainable energy plan) enters its fourth year, with energy security pressures accelerating cross-border grid coordination. It is expected that grid connection cycles in core regions will be shortened by 10-20% by 2026, but structural barriers in land planning and cross-border coordination will continue to limit overall acceleration.

Although India set a record for new renewable energy additions in 2025, only about 58% of the transmission system construction target was achieved. In August 2025, CERC (Central Electricity Regulatory Commission) issued the third amendment to the GNA (General Network Access) regulations, introducing time-of-day access mechanisms for solar and non-solar hours, extending the ISTS (Inter-State Transmission System) fee waiver period for storage, and piloting single-window approvals and time-of-day channel optimization, aiming to improve utilization of the existing network. However, difficulties in land acquisition and weak distribution infrastructure remain unresolved in the short term, and overall expansion speed is still constrained by execution gaps at the implementation level.
