# **II. Market Landscape and Key Players in PD-(L)1/VEGF Bispecifics**

**According to global pharma financial reports, the market size for immune checkpoint inhibitors exceeded USD 60 billion in 2025. The PD-1 inhibitor segment is dominated by two players: Merck’s Keytruda and BMS’s Opdivo, with 2025 sales of USD 31.7 billion and USD 11.0 billion, respectively. Their core patents will expire around 2028.**

In September 2024, Akeso Biopharma presented impressive HARMONi-2 clinical results at WCLC. Its ivonescimab (AK112, PD-1/VEGF) outperformed Keytruda in head-to-head Phase III for PD-1-positive NSCLC: mPFS 11.1 months vs. Keytruda’s 5.8 months, PFS HR=0.51; cORR 50.0% vs. Keytruda’s 38.5%. This ignited significant industry enthusiasm, with expectations that PD-1/VEGF bispecifics could become the next generation of cancer immunotherapy, gradually capturing the massive USD 60 billion+ market.

The author believes the market opportunity for PD-1/VEGF bispecifics lies in: 1) improving efficacy over existing immunotherapies, potentially achieving synergistic efficacy and improved safety through dual targeting; 2) exploring indications unresponsive to current immunotherapies.

**According to BioNTech, PD-1 therapies have been approved for about half of cancer patients in Europe and the US (1.5 million new cases annually), including major indications such as NSCLC (PD-L1 positive), SCLC, liver cancer, and gastric cancer (PD-L1 positive). However, a large number of patients remain unresponsive to PD-1 therapy, with 1.4 million new cases annually in Europe and the US. Major indications include breast cancer (non-TNBC), CRC (MSS), EGFR-mutant NSCLC, pancreatic cancer, etc.**

In May 2025, Pfizer signed a major licensing agreement with 3SBIO (1530 HK) totaling over USD 6 billion, including an upfront payment of USD 1.25 billion. Pfizer obtained global development, manufacturing, and commercialization rights for SSGJ-707 (subsequently PF‘4404).

Current leading players in the PD-1/VEGF bispecific field also include Akeso Biopharma, BioNTech, and EMD Serono/Instil Bio, among others.

**Akeso Biopharma is the most advanced.**

Ivonescimab was approved in China in May 2024 for use with chemotherapy in EGFR-mutant non-squamous NSCLC after EGFR TKI progression. In April 2025, it was further approved for first-line monotherapy in PD-L1-positive NSCLC, with pivotal evidence from randomized studies such as Phase III HARMONi-A (AK112-301) and HARMONi-2 (AK112-303). In head-to-head HARMONi-2, ivonescimab monotherapy significantly prolonged PFS versus pembrolizumab (mPFS 11.14 vs. 5.82 months; HR 0.51), with higher ORR (50.0% vs. 38.5%) and overall manageable safety; it is also being advanced in China with priority review. Other late-stage core indications are also in NSCLC.

**In September 2025, Summit (Akeso’s overseas partner for AK112) presented clinical data for first-line squamous NSCLC (HARMONi-3) at ESMO. The primary endpoint PFS was positive, HR=0.52; the secondary endpoint OS was not yet mature but showed an improving trend (interim analysis α allocation only 0.0001), though not statistically significant. OS HR=0.78.**

Market debate around its clinical data centers on:

a) Some believe PFS improvement is mainly due to early VEGF inhibition, and ultimate OS benefit may be limited. In US/EU regulatory systems, OS is the gold standard and core for approval and pricing. There are doubts about whether it will ultimately be approved in the US.

b) Although HARMONi is global and multicenter, 62% of enrollment was in China and 38% in the US/EU. Western markets discount data primarily generated in China.

**BioNTech’s BNT327 (pumitamig) is a PD-L1/VEGF-A bispecific. It leads in SCLC and TNBC, both having entered Phase III in 2025.**

The global Phase II ES-SCLC trial (NCT06449209) showed strong early signals (cORR 76.3%, mPFS 6.8 months), supporting multiple global late-stage studies with registration potential, such as ROSETTA Lung01 (ES-SCLC, NCT06712355) and ROSETTA Lung02 (NSCLC, NCT06712316). In June 2025, BioNTech and BMS entered a global co-development and commercialization partnership (50/50 profit and cost sharing), significantly strengthening global clinical and commercial resources.

**Market concerns for Pfizer center on the fact that PF‘4404’s competitors are ahead in the PD-1/VEGF field. How will Pfizer differentiate and catch up? The author believes Pfizer’s first-tier multinational R&D resources, speed, rigorous clinical design, and differentiation should not be underestimated.**

**Overall, PF‘4404’s development strategy is “late mover but not conservative.” After seeing the PD-1/VEGF bispecific mechanism validated by ivonescimab, Pfizer rapidly positioned PF‘4404 as a “pipeline-in-a-product.” The approach is to first tackle indications that can quickly establish clinical and commercial anchors (such as NSCLC), then expand to more tumor types and combination regimens. Pfizer itself summarizes this approach as threefold: establishing backbone therapy across multiple tumor types, launching 7 near-term trials, and considering over 10 additional indications and 10+ combinations. This reflects a development strategy that combines speed and breadth. The following two major sections will focus on Pfizer’s differentiated clinical development path and the NSCLC and mCRC indications.**

# **III. Pfizer’s Differentiated Development Strategy for PF‘4404**

Against the backdrop of a USD 60 billion market for PD-1/VEGF bispecifics and competitors’ first-mover advantages, Pfizer has leveraged its R&D resources and clinical design capabilities to formulate a “speed + breadth + depth” differentiated development strategy for PF-08634404.

PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 completed the transaction in July 2025, and after communication with the US FDA, Pfizer launched two pivotal Phase III trials (NSCLC, mCRC) in the US within just three months. The company also announced plans to add over 10 new indications and multiple combination regimens in 2026. This demonstrates Pfizer’s execution efficiency. All announced clinical trials use OS as the primary endpoint or OS/PFS dual primary endpoints, and all are head-to-head against current standard of care, reflecting rigorous clinical design.

**In terms of indication selection, lung cancer is the main battlefield for PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecifics, as validated by both capital markets and the clinical community. Akeso’s strategy was to first select PD-L1-positive NSCLC patients and, in an area where immunotherapy has an advantage, beat Keytruda head-to-head.**

**Pfizer’s approach is not simple followership. Pfizer is conducting a global Phase III trial covering the entire NSCLC population (PD-L1 positive or negative) for first-line treatment, aiming not just for a single clinical success but to establish a long-term standard of care. The trial is head-to-head against Keytruda + chemotherapy, covering both squamous (SQ) and non-squamous (NSQ) NSCLC populations, supporting potential registration (details in the NSCLC section below).**

**In SCLC, BioNTech is ahead, but Pfizer emphasizes clear unmet clinical needs. Therefore, it is conducting a seamless Phase II/III design, head-to-head against Tecentriq + chemotherapy, aiming to support sBLA (accelerated approval) with a single large trial.**

The first-line standard for SCLC is Roche’s Tecentriq (atezolizumab) + chemotherapy, which offers limited long-term OS improvement and widespread resistance. According to the IMpower133 Phase III trial: the experimental group (Tecentriq + chemotherapy) had mOS of 12.3 months vs. 10.3 months for the control group (placebo + chemotherapy). HR=0.7, reducing the risk of death by 30%.

**In mCRC (MSS/pMMR), PD-1 monotherapy is not standard of care, but anti-VEGF agents such as bevacizumab are. Pfizer’s Phase III design is PF‘4404 + mFOLFOX6 versus bevacizumab + mFOLFOX6, explicitly excluding MSI-H/dMMR patients (for whom PD-1 monotherapy is already standard). Pfizer’s logic is clear: rather than going head-to-head with PD-1 monotherapy, it aims to upgrade the accepted “VEGF + chemotherapy” standard to “PD-1/VEGF bispecific + chemotherapy.” This is more aligned with real-world clinical decision-making than simply substituting in immuno-sensitive tumor types.**

Additionally, Pfizer places combinations with its own vedotin ADC at the core. In its own words: “Build chemo-free or chemo-sparing backbone regimens leveraging bispecifics and ADC combinations.”

From an industry perspective, ADCs are increasingly showing “chemo-substitution” potential in some indications: on one hand, ADCs such as Enhertu and Trodelvy have achieved high ORR in multiple tumor types; on the other, combinations with immunotherapy are showing preliminary synergy, providing a practical basis for “chemo-free” or “chemo-sparing” regimens.

**Against this backdrop, Pfizer’s strategy is not simply to stack new mechanisms, but to attempt to reconstruct the first-line treatment framework: centering on PD-1/VEGF bispecific + ADC combinations to explore alternatives to current immunotherapy + chemotherapy standards such as Keytruda + chemotherapy.**

**Pfizer held analyst meetings on PF‘4404 clinical development in July and November 2025. The author reviews the key disclosed points below.**   
1. Pfizer emphasizes its strong presence in thoracic, reproductive, and gastrointestinal oncology, as well as its technical expertise in small molecules, ADCs, and bispecific/multispecifics. PF‘4404 is highly synergistic with Pfizer’s oncology business. Officially, it is described as “backbone therapy,” underscoring Pfizer’s commitment to PF‘4404.

**2. Pfizer estimates that PF‘4404 could reach over 350,000 eligible new patients annually in the US, with major indications including: Nsq NSCLC (non-squamous NSCLC) 104,000, Sq NSCLC (squamous NSCLC) 37,000, UC (urothelial carcinoma) 19,000, RCC (renal cell carcinoma) 16,000, and mCRC (metastatic colorectal cancer) 63,000. 3SBIO has already conducted Phase I/II trials for PF‘4404 in Nsq NSCLC, Sq NSCLC, and CRC in China.**   
3. This is also reflected in robust clinical resource investment. As of November 2025, Pfizer plans to launch seven global clinical trials in the near term, including two Phase III trials; as shown in Chart 2, these include 1L Sq and Nsq NSCLC Phase III and 1L mCRC. In addition, Pfizer plans to initiate another 10 indications and over 10 combination regimens (including ADCs) by the end of 2026.   
4. Regarding why SSGJ-707 was chosen among global BD targets, PF‘4404 franchise head Arati Rao did not disclose much detail. The main reason is its tetrabody structure, which allows each arm to simultaneously bind PD-1 and VEGF. In the presence of VEGF, SSGJ-707 forms multimers. **This multimerization/daisy chaining effect significantly enhances PD-1 affinity, ultimately increasing PD-1 binding nearly tenfold.**   
5. Analysts asked about safety data for SSGJ-707 at ASCO, where Grade ≥3 TRAEs were 39% vs. 24% in the mid-year update. There were also three patient deaths, two from hemoptysis. Arati Rao responded that this may be due to enrolling patients with recent radiotherapy history without a washout period. Such issues can be avoided with standardized clinical procedures going forward.   
6. Pfizer stated that prior to Phase III, dose selection followed the FDA Project Optimus approach, using precise quantitative benefit-risk assessment instead of traditional maximum tolerated dose development. While this increases sample monitoring, real-time analysis, and early development complexity, it helps achieve a better benefit-risk profile.

**The author believes that, whether in preemptive handling of NSCLC histological differences, dose optimization, or standardized clinical operations, Pfizer demonstrates leading FDA communication and rigorous clinical design capabilities.**

# **V. PF‘4404 Demonstrates BIC Potential in First-Line NSCLC**

According to the latest clinicaltrials.gov data, Pfizer’s 1L Sq and Nsq NSCLC Phase III trial is enrolling patients (NCT07222566, Symbiotic-Lung-01). The trial has dual primary endpoints of OS (estimated follow-up 39 months) and PFS (estimated follow-up 32 months), with initial data readout expected in February 2029.

**According to Pfizer’s presentation, there are 104,000 new Nsq NSCLC and 37,000 new Sq NSCLC cases annually in the US. A highlight of Pfizer’s development is preemptive handling of histological differences. SQ and NSQ NSCLC use different chemotherapy backbones, better reflecting real-world clinical practice.**

Per Pfizer’s ELCC 2026 presentation, the clinical design is as follows:

Part 1: ~640 squamous NSCLC patients, comparing PF‘4404 or pembrolizumab plus carboplatin + paclitaxel/nab-paclitaxel.

Part 2: ~770 non-squamous NSCLC patients, comparing PF‘4404 or pembrolizumab plus carboplatin + pemetrexed, with pemetrexed maintenance in the latter.

In other words, it does not apply a one-size-fits-all chemotherapy regimen to all NSCLC, but incorporates standard chemotherapy backbones for different histologies within the same Phase III framework.

**Advantages in development include:**

**1) Both control and experimental arms better reflect real-world standard of care:** SQ and NSQ patients in clinical practice receive different standard chemotherapy combinations and maintenance strategies, with differing toxicity and benefit profiles.

**2) Clearer statistical and regulatory narratives:** Pfizer states, “all endpoints will be assessed in each part.” Rather than combining both populations for a single primary endpoint and then doing subgroup analyses, each part is an independent analysis unit with its own endpoints, yielding clear, population-specific conclusions rather than being tied to a mixed overall result.

**3) Avoiding subsequent bridging studies and accelerating progress:** Some trials first target a population likely to yield positive results, then conduct bridging studies to extend the label to other histologies. However, bridging is often inefficient, adding time, cost, and regulatory scrutiny.

**The author compared clinical trials for PF‘4404/SSGJ-707, ivonescimab, and BNT327.**

**In terms of ORR, PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 achieved 64.7%, higher than ivonescimab’s 50.0% and BNT327’s 47.0%.**

For TRAEs, PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 had Grade ≥3 TRAEs in 23.5%, slightly lower than ivonescimab’s 29.4%. BNT327’s Grade ≥3 TRAEs were 19.7%, but this included other subgroups (EGFR-mutant, treatment-refractory) and not just PD-L1-positive, EGFR/ALK wild-type patients. BNT327’s discontinuation rate due to TRAEs was 8.2%, significantly higher than PF‘4404/SSGJ-707’s 2.9% and ivonescimab’s 2.2%.

**Phase II data indicate that PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 demonstrates efficacy and safety advantages, showing BIC potential.**

# **VI. Significant Unmet Need in CRC (MSS/pMMR); PD-1/VEGF Bispecifics Poised to Replace and Upgrade “Chemotherapy + VEGF” Standard of Care**

According to the latest clinicaltrials.gov data, Pfizer’s Phase III trial for first-line mCRC (MSS/pMMR) is enrolling patients (NCT07222800, Symbiotic-GI-03). The trial has dual primary endpoints of OS and PFS, with an estimated 4-year follow-up. Initial data readout is expected in March 2030.

**Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide. According to Pfizer’s presentation, there are 63,000 new mCRC cases annually in the US.**

**MSI-H/dMMR patients (5%) are classic immuno-sensitive subtypes, with first-line therapy having shifted from traditional chemotherapy to PD-1 inhibitor-based immunotherapy, and some patients receiving combination immunotherapy. However, about 95% of CRC is “microsatellite stable” (MSS/pMMR), representing a significant unmet need. MSS tumors are not only immunosuppressed (PD-1 pathway activation), but also secrete large amounts of VEGF, forming abnormal vasculature that blocks immune cell infiltration. Thus, these tumors are nearly unresponsive to mainstream PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitors and are termed “immune cold tumors.”**

**In MSS/pMMR mCRC, first-line standard of care is chemotherapy-based, combined with anti-VEGF or anti-EGFR targeted therapy, with “chemotherapy + bevacizumab” being one of the most widely used regimens. The rationale for PD-1/VEGF bispecifics is to address both “immune activation” and “immune infiltration” bottlenecks: VEGF inhibition normalizes vasculature and remodels the microenvironment, while PD-1 blockade lifts immune suppression, potentially converting “cold” to “hot” tumors. Compared to traditional PD-1 + VEGF combinations, bispecifics achieve spatial and temporal synergy at the molecular level, theoretically offering better local exposure and synergy.**

1) RAS-mutant: largest subgroup, resistant to EGFR-targeted and immunotherapies. Opportunity lies in replacing traditional “bevacizumab + chemotherapy.”

2) Right-sided CRC (RAS wild-type): poor EGFR response, opportunity to replace bevacizumab.

3) BRAF-mutant: poor prognosis, limited treatment options. Potential for supplementary or earlier-line therapy.

In terms of efficacy, PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 achieved an overall ORR of 65.5%, with confirmed ORR of 57.5%. For comparison, bevacizumab’s ORR was 35.3%. Ivonescimab’s ORR was slightly higher, with 81.8% and 88.2% in groups A and B, respectively.

For safety, PF‘4404/SSGJ-707 had Grade ≥3 TRAEs in 43.2%, slightly better than ivonescimab group A (54.5%), group B (44.4%), and the bevacizumab arm (69%).
