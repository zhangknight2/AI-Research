**What was the average increase level of Alibaba Cloud's compute price hike yesterday? Was it mainly driven by cost or by demand?**

Alibaba Cloud only announced price increases for several major items this time, but in reality there are more than fifty items involved, divided into several major categories. The cloud host increase is 5%, and this also includes elastic computing, containers, function computing, databases, data warehouses, PAI, storage, MaxCompute, Bailian platform, and other products. The smallest increase is 5%, and the highest is for data warehouses, ranging from 25–34%. Overall, data-related products saw larger increases, software-category products had higher increases, and hardware-category products had lower increases, with hardware roughly increasing around 5%. Alibaba Cloud's pricing strategy is small increases on hardware and large increases on software. The core logic is that hardware is affected by the supply chain; increases in storage and other hardware are mainly passed through from the supply chain. Elastic computing and CPU demand are very strong — Agent deployment requires a large number of CPU servers — so demand-side growth has driven software-category price increases, which are ultimately supported by hardware. For example, uniform memory, data warehouses, and other data-related products all require storage support behind them, so while software products show surface-level price increases, hardware products actually have hidden price increases as well. Alibaba Cloud did not directly announce storage price increases but transmitted them through database product increases. The core reason is still the supply chain pass-through effect. External supply chain price increases have continued for two to three quarters; after the reorganization they had not raised prices until now, and the time had come where they had to raise them. The increases started from April 18. In addition, resource supply on the demand side remains tight — both CPU and compute are in a state of supply short of demand — so this price increase is relatively reasonable.

**Why did the data warehouse see the highest increase?**

Taking Cloud Database RDS as an example, RDS is mainly used to store structured data, including text, images, etc., and ultimately all of it needs to be stored through cloud databases. The cloud-native multimodal database increase is 25–34%, mainly used in the autonomous driving industry. Data collected by car cameras needs to be stored for autonomous driving training. Currently many automotive industry customers are working on autonomous driving, leading to a substantial increase in demand for data storage and training. MaxCompute's cloud-native big data computing service also saw price increases because the inference process requires capturing and computing large amounts of dynamic feature data. Related products on the Bailian platform saw lower increases on the international site and 20–30% increases on the China site, mainly due to rising API call costs and compute rental prices. Overall, the data warehouse price increase is mainly due to industry demand growth, especially the increased demand for data storage and processing brought by autonomous driving and big data computing scenarios.

**Including consideration of Wukong's token commercial consumption, etc., will there be further price increases in the next two quarters?**

It is expected that there will continue to be price increases within the year. This time they only raised by 5% first. With the expansion of the OpenClaw product line, the company mainly focuses on to-B and C-end products. Wukong, as a collaboration tool, can automatically pass through to enterprise users via the DingTalk entrance, bringing a large amount of incremental traffic. After enterprises purchase Wukong, the traffic behind it requires additional payment, and the corresponding databases, storage, and compute will all grow accordingly. In the future Wukong will charge based on usage, and there will be many skills on the platform; high user volume will be charged according to actual traffic, which requires a large amount of CPU servers, storage, and database product support behind it. Price increase frequency will not be once per quarter; once every two quarters is more reasonable.

**If there are two price increases this year (2026), will the impact on the gross margin and operating profit margin of the Cloud Intelligence segment be very obvious? Or will the impact be small?**

Single-digit increases at the IaaS layer have a relatively small impact on overall revenue. Large database products fall under the PaaS layer, accounting for about 25–30% of Alibaba overall. With a 34% increase multiplied by the proportion, it affects roughly 8–9 percentage points.

**What are Alibaba's token hub considerations that combine the strategies of 5 BUs? What improvements will there be in operational efficiency and product launches?**

Previously each business line operated independently — building models, building applications, building MaaS — each with its own goals, leading to uneven allocation of compute resources and low collaboration efficiency. The application side pursued DAU, the model side pursued performance and cost, and the MaaS side pursued sales; goals were not unified. Now it has shifted to token orientation, aligning goals by categorizing token production, transmission, and consumption. For example, Tongyi Lab + Alibaba Cloud handles Infra, focusing on improving token intelligent applications so tokens run faster and cheaper. The application side is responsible for scenario implementation, and the MaaS side is responsible for empowering enterprises. They set one overall goal — for example, 200 trillion daily average tokens by the end of this year (2026) — and business departments hope to minimize costs; the model side and Infra side must meet the low-cost target and then pass it to the business side. C-end and B-end consume tokens, and the target is distributed across different business lines, forming a collaborative management system built around the token value chain. March marks the end of FY2026 and the start of FY2027; when setting OKRs, goals are aligned to form upstream-downstream relationships. For example, the General Lab previously aimed to lead in model rankings; now it must include OKRs related to token production. This year (2026) the cost per million tokens needs to be driven down by how much, the large model release rhythm needs to be clarified, the model side is responsible for production, Infra is responsible for compute support, the business side is responsible for token production and consumption, the application layer plans daily active levels, task completion rates, average tokens produced per task, and Wukong also needs to break down targets. Everyone builds goals around token production, transmission, and consumption, which improves management collaboration efficiency.

**What is the model combination used in Wukong's overall process? Does it only use Qwen models, or does it support all third-party models?**

Wukong is one type within the OpenClaw product matrix. Architecturally, models are decoupled from agent skills; by default it uses Qwen models. Enterprise users can use it through the Wukong Windows client or DingTalk entrance; the DingTalk entrance defaults to the Qwen series. The overall architecture supports open access — for example, it can be configured with Kimi, Zhipu, and other third-party models — and will not lock users into only using Alibaba models. All models are deployed on Alibaba Cloud; regardless of which company's model it is, the compute is provided by Alibaba Cloud, so model diversity is allowed.

**How many skills does Wukong have? How does it cooperate with third-party partners to develop them?**

Wukong has its own agent skill system, including the Alibaba ecosystem — from payments and e-commerce to agent/skills accumulated in DingTalk. Currently there are about 600+ in-house ecosystem skills, plus third-party ones totaling about 1,100+, for a combined total of over 1,900. Skills are divided into ten major official skills and four or five sub-skills. After each skill goes live it must be verified and screened, forming an architecture that is mainly self-developed with third-party as supplementary. The platform encourages high-quality third-party skill access but requires certification and review to ensure safety and reliability. Scenarios are divided into ten major categories, including domestic e-commerce, cross-border e-commerce, content creation, software development, store management, intelligent customer service, inventory management, operations assistant, design (e.g., renovation posters), manufacturing (supply chain, quality inspection, equipment maintenance), legal documents, finance, HR, headhunting, etc. Each scenario has dominant core skills and will also access third-party skills, managed by scenario category.

**How long have the 1,000+ skills already been in development? What will the growth rhythm be going forward?**

Actually it has already been worked on for two quarters. This is benchmarking against OpenClaw. OpenClaw uses an IM-style way to command agents; Wukong's skills have CLI — equivalent to making skills CLI-based, so below you can control Skills by typing commands. Going forward it will open the ecosystem and not make it closed-loop. The reason it is called Wukong is the hope to dominate the B-end agent direction — this is a relatively big vision. But it has just come out, so we still need to watch the next few months. This market already has the OpenClaw series of products; its strength is relying on the strong DingTalk entrance to push it to some users, but it will also face competition from WeChat, Feishu, etc.

**In these two quarters, have we seen these skills significantly increase token consumption, or do skills actually optimize token consumption?**

Right after release there is a free quota; new users basically get 5 million tokens each. During this period we can get a rough sense: skill generation of simple, medium, and complex tasks — high-frequency simple tasks consume roughly over 10,000 tokens per day, medium tasks about 50,000, complex tasks basically 100,000. The breakdown is 42% simple tasks, around 38% medium, and 20% complex. Going forward users will focus more on medium-to-complex levels, exceeding 40–50%. The overall scenario will further open up and basically cover most things that can be done in an office — unless something extra is needed that isn't there, then another agent can supplement. Most office tasks can be covered. Currently one customer consumes about 30–40,000 tokens per day. Right now many customers are still learning how to use it; once proficient, each customer will orchestrate skills themselves to write, and when they are not at the office or before arriving they will start the agent to work for them — by the time they get to the office things are already done. For example, if there is a meeting in the afternoon, they throw the task to the agent in the morning, and by noon the PPT is written and they can take the PPT to the meeting.

**What is the internal test effect of Wukong? Accuracy rate, hallucination performance, etc.?**

Wukong has a relatively strong base — Alibaba Cloud specifically has an AgentBay dedicated to its entire Infra, including created sandboxes, containers, etc. Running on that infrastructure is relatively efficient. Products like OpenClaw — the most complex ones — if run locally would have data leakage, token explosion, and other issues. Before release we did a large amount of testing; internally it has already been used for more than a month. There are good and bad aspects. The good part is the development system — integrating our code and Lingma, while also allowing calls to third-party cloud models — making it relatively easy for the development industry to get started. For example, when doing product work you often need to write documents, draw diagrams, design — including writing product PRDs, drawing diagrams, writing PPTs, generating accompanying images — the whole process is quite smooth. Areas that are not yet good enough are oriented toward manufacturing and stores and still need optimization. Covering the ten major scenarios will be further expanded; it has not yet achieved 100% penetration. Subsequently high-quality Skills will be selected to enrich it. The opening has already started — from December the product has already been passed through to existing enterprise customers, so customer acquisition is relatively fast. It is not expanding customers from 0 to 1; basically DingTalk customers who upgrade versions will see the entrance.

**What is Alibaba's commission method for third-party skills? Will it be difficult for it to make a meaningful contribution to intelligent cloud revenue this year (2026)?**

Right now basically March and April — this one-plus month — are encouraging customers to use it; there is a period of free use first. Original enterprise DingTalk customers will not be charged extra because of this. After the free trial period, it will be paid according to skill traffic. Customers can buy packages — for example, an enterprise with 1,000 users might buy 1 trillion tokens for the year, purchasing total tokens from which employee consumption is deducted. They can also buy packages with certain volume constraints — for example, daily or monthly total limits — and use within the limit. After the free period, metering and billing rules will be formulated.

**When will charging start? Will it start this year (2026)?**

Right now it is in trial operation; trial operation is generally 2–3 months, so by May–June it will start commercialization, at the latest late June or July.

**Recent linkage between CoPaw and Wukong?**

What Jin said is that before ATH was established, every team was doing it, so Alibaba had several OpenClaw products: CoPaw, HitPaw, JVS Claw, plus Wukong — four in total. CoPaw is the product made by the General Lab's agent team; originally the Communications Lab had a dedicated team doing Agentscope that was previously for customers to build and follow up on their own products. They released a QQ version that became the personal version, with desktop and web versions, allowing connection to one's own models and third-party models, biased toward individual developers who can configure external open-source ones. Wukong's skills are strictly controlled for quality and not casually connected; it is enterprise-grade, so they are different.

**What is Wukong's global market strategy and rhythm?**

Wukong is currently mainly being promoted domestically; main customers are still concentrated in China. Overseas it has to compete with Slack and other overseas IMs; the advantage is still domestic. Domestically it mainly relies on the DingTalk entrance. Among the three major domestic IMs — DingTalk, Enterprise WeChat, Feishu — DingTalk ranks first. Using Wukong to reactivate DingTalk's energy. Previously DingTalk was mainly paid by subscription with many built-in AI functions packaged together; some customers paid the subscription fee whether they used it or not. Now customers demand pay-per-use, and with Wukong this problem is solved. Buy basic subscription fees, and in the future with so many skills, pay for what you use — relatively flexible, pay according to actual usage and traffic, which is fairer to users. When DingTalk does products, so many skills can be combined into numerous product matrices, reducing shopping-cart customization work and playing a big role in expanding enterprise customers. DingTalk has more than 200 million enterprise users; assuming 20% use Wukong, that's 40 million customers using it, forming incremental token consumption.

**Is there still any compute tightness at Alibaba recently? Considering the recent reorganization and Wukong launch, will the compute card and capex expectations for this year (2026) and next year be raised?**

Existing chips are limited; new cards have not come up so quickly yet. They need to tap compute resource efficiency through the ATH system architecture. Overall cards are currently in a relatively tight state. NVIDIA cannot come in — at least not yet; there is still uncertainty in the next quarter, and budget will reserve a portion for NVIDIA. This year (2026) resource cards need to accelerate; both resource cards and survival-oriented cards need to speed up. From March they are fully testing 950 chip generation supplementation. This year (2026) domestic cards are the main volume supply; resource cards accelerate mass production. Domestically dominant; buy NVIDIA if possible, if not then shift focus to leasing and overseas supplementary compute. Medium-to-long term, as long as resource cards and domestic cards start up, there is large room to operate. It depends on the supply chain situation; currently no capex adjustment has been seen.

**How do you evaluate the progress and strategies of other model companies in enterprise Agents?**

Everyone has seen the trend is to make OpenClaw a strong entrance — especially Tencent with WeChat will definitely do it early. In the battle, Tencent may move faster although its model is not that strong yet. Now WorkBuddy and these QQ products are decoupled; the model below does not necessarily have to use their own Hunyuan and can use DeepSeek to hold for a while first. Strongly bound to WeChat ecosystem, and with Mini Programs + Enterprise WeChat it can deploy quickly. Tencent has an advantage doing low-end products and can quickly reach through existing customers. WeChat may move fastest in the battle; big factories rank first in customer acquisition and ecosystem construction speed. ByteDance also has its own Claw — Lark overseas, Feishu domestically — and will also penetrate B-end enterprise social customers through this entrance. Domestic big factories will all re-examine the value of OpenClaw entrances and will go all out. If cloud loses the OpenClaw market, it will be hard to catch up later. Now is early stage; once user habits form during customer acquisition, they won't want to switch later. So Alibaba, ByteDance, and Doubao will definitely deeply cultivate OpenClaw products, forming strong matrices and entrance control capabilities. Kimi and Zhipu are also doing it, but their natural weakness is having no entrance product, so their market share will be smaller. However, in the future they can create some skills and then integrate into other big factory ecosystems.

**What are the coping strategies of other e-commerce platforms without mature models — for example JD, Meituan — in response to the market now?**

Pinduoduo is in observation mode; it has not made big moves whether in model testing or anything else, adopting a relatively conservative strategy of following behind others. This reduces resource waste from stepping on pits early. In the past two years we have not seen it buy large amounts of compute or build teams, but it has a research group long-term tracking the industry; currently it has not yet exerted force in AI and is guarding its own boundary.

Meituan is relatively passive, suppressed by Flash Purchase; in 2026 Alibaba hopes to exceed Meituan's market share. Meituan is under great pressure. It does not have anything particularly outstanding in AI — although it open-sourced some models, iteration speed and model quality are still weak — only using models to empower its own business scenarios and having agents embedded, but actions are not big enough.

JD is more active than these two; it started in 2024–2025 and this year (2026) also wants to do guides at the entrance. JD AI's main strategy is using AI to optimize the experience of existing end products, also supporting universal search products similar to parsing, and using AI to reconstruct commodity control, aiming at being able to commercialize commodity search through AI search and AA docking in the future. JD may cooperate with ByteDance or Tencent in the future, connecting its commodity library external agents to others' ecosystems to be called at the base level; it has no main entrance and is mainly guarding its own agent — this is the strategy.

**Does the recent business reorganization and team adjustments mean that base model iteration will slow down later? What is the new model strategy and rhythm this year (2026)?**

Main impacts include: First, the Qwen Max flagship new model press conference is delayed; originally prepared to wait for DeepSeek V4, expected to release in late March–early April. Assuming V4 releases early next month, Qwen Max new version will be delayed by about one and a half to two months. Second, Qwen 3.5 Coder model will also be postponed. If Qwen 3 Max releases in May, then one more month later — June or July — before releasing the Coder model. Originally Qwen 4 was to be released in Q2; now delayed to late September before the Yunqi Conference, about two to two-and-a-half months later than planned. After the March business reorganization, the teams were quickly connected; old employees lead pre-training, post-training, code, etc., to achieve a smooth transition. This adjustment is only short-term pain; long-term it is good. Previously models were released individually — language models, visual understanding, visual generation, etc. — and teams were also separate, each lab having independent data engineering, pre-training, post-training. Now models need to move toward fusion, so people need to be pulled to do the middle platform; algorithm teams should be merged into multimodal teams — otherwise coordination and communication across teams become complex during model fusion. Short-term pain, long-term benefit.
