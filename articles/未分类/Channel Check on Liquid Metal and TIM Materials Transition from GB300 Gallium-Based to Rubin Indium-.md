At the Rubin stage, indium-based liquid metal is adopted to avoid corrosion and leakage issues. In terms of thermal conductivity: thermal grease is in the teens, gallium-based is 60–70, indium-based is 30–40, sufficient for 2,300W GPUs.

**Expert Opinions:**

**Bryce: Could you briefly introduce the basic concepts of liquid metal, TIM1, TIM1.5, and TIM2?**

Expert: Currently, liquid metal is mainly used in server cooling. The common material is thermal grease, but due to the high heat output of NVIDIA server GPUs—often exceeding 800W—thermal grease efficiency drops sharply and cannot meet requirements, leading to the adoption of liquid metal. GB300 initially used thermal grease for validation during prototyping, but by May 2025, mass production had largely switched to gallium-based liquid metal. This material is moderately priced but corrosive to copper and aluminum, so GB300 uses nickel-plated cold plates for protection. Although GB300 uses gallium-based liquid metal, it is considered a transitional product.

At the Rubin production stage, indium-based liquid metal is introduced. Indium-based liquid metal is more expensive and has slightly lower thermal conductivity, but it does not cause corrosion or structural damage and avoids leakage issues. The maximum thermal conductivity of thermal grease is in the teens—leading companies can achieve 11 or 12, while others reach 7 or 8. Gallium-based liquid metal can reach 60–70, offering good performance, but due to corrosion, it remains a transitional solution. Indium-based liquid metal has a conductivity of 30–40, sufficient for 2,300W GPUs.

TIM1 typically refers to the layer of thermal grease on the chip, including subsequent gallium-based liquid metal layers. TIM2 is an additional layer to handle higher heat loads, as TIM1 alone cannot dissipate heat quickly enough. TIM1.5 generally refers to a pure copper lid on the chip, which is less common.

**Bryce: With the trend of co-packaged optics (CPO), overall network cluster power consumption may decrease, but does this affect the need for material upgrades at the chip level? Will switch chips see increased power consumption? Besides GPUs, what is the current status for switch chips, and when might they adopt these materials?**

Expert: The CPO process will indirectly increase switch chip power consumption, driving demand for upgraded liquid cooling materials between chips. All-optical communication is the future trend, as high-speed copper cables are limited to distances under 3 meters and may be replaced. All-optical communication will increase switch chip power, potentially accelerating liquid cooling material upgrades.

Bryce: Are there customers in the supply chain expressing demand for liquid metal, such as for future CPO switch chips?

Expert: Not at this time.

**Bryce: Besides NVIDIA, are there other customers—such as Google or Meta—showing demand for liquid metal?**

Expert: The main consideration is chip power consumption. Google has confirmed adoption of liquid metal, and H Company may gradually apply it as well. Liquid metal is mainly used for high-power chips like CPUs and GPUs, especially under full load and extreme conditions.

Bryce: Which generation did Google start using it?

Expert: V8. The inference series chips have lower power and have not adopted it yet. Not all training chips use liquid metal—about 30% of training chips do, and penetration is gradually increasing.

Bryce: Do domestic customers like H Company have demand for liquid metal? What is the current progress?

Expert: H Company may adopt liquid metal in future iterations. Currently, we mainly supply thermal grease, and demand is limited, but a switch is likely in the future. Liquid metal helps improve CPU and GPU operating temperature and lifespan, especially under full load and extreme conditions.

Bryce: Logically, I understand, but are there any signs in the industry or actual cases of ASIC customers adopting it? You mentioned Google is only in contact, so there are no orders or forecasts yet, correct?

Expert: We are in contact, but there are no orders yet—just small batches for sampling.

**Bryce: Regarding TAM, what is the liquid metal market size for Rubin and GB300? What is the per-card value for liquid metal materials?**

Expert: GB300 currently ships about 4,000–5,000 units per month. After mass production in June/July 2025, monthly shipments were 2,000–3,000; since January 2026, monthly shipments are 5,000–6,000. The TIM1 liquid metal material value per GPU is about RMB 130–140 (about $20). Rubin requires an additional TIM2 layer, with TIM1 valued at about RMB 300 and TIM2 at about RMB 500—these are incremental values. The value refers to the material alone, excluding supporting components like cold plates.

Bryce: Is it also used for CPUs?

Expert: Yes, CPUs use it as well. The value is similar but slightly lower, around RMB 100.

**Bryce: After discussing the core material value, you mentioned that supporting materials are needed. How should we view and estimate these supporting materials?**

Expert: GB300 only has one TIM1 layer. Rubin adds a TIM2 layer, and the cold plate value changes as well. For GB300, a tray contains 6 cold plates, each about $300. For Rubin, there are 2 cold plates per tray, each about $850. Although the per-plate value increases, the overall value decreases slightly.

**Bryce: Besides cold plate upgrades, you mentioned supporting materials like sealing rings to prevent metal leakage. What is the incremental value—can you give a percentage, such as a 20–30% increase on an RMB 800 base?**

Expert: Yes, there are sealing rings. These supporting materials are not expensive, with an incremental value of about 5%. They are mainly special rubber rings—raw materials are cheap, and technical content is low, with some special additives in the formula to improve aging resistance. Overall, these supporting materials have high margins but limited value.

Bryce: Is the total value for all these components about RMB 40?

Expert: Roughly, yes. Mainly rubber, with high margins but low raw material cost and technical content.

Bryce: The 5% you mentioned—does it refer to sealing rings only, or does it include all supporting upgrades, such as pads and gels for HBM, switches, and memory?

Expert: It includes all supporting components. The overall value is not particularly high.

Bryce: Is the supporting value for CPUs or switch chips also estimated at 5%, or does it vary?

Expert: Also estimated at 5%.

**Bryce: Are there price differences between domestic and overseas suppliers?**

Expert: Domestic suppliers are similar in price; overseas suppliers are 20%–30% higher.

**Bryce: Is there any additional information needed to clarify the market landscape?**

Expert: Focus on GB300 and Rubin shipment volumes. GB300 should remain the main product in 2026, with monthly shipments of at least 5,000 units, possibly higher. Rubin will have little impact on GB300 this year, with monthly shipments of 1,000–2,000 in the second half, possibly affecting the market. By next year (2027), Rubin could account for 40%–50%; in the second half of this year, it may reach 10%–20% in a given month. Rubin's total shipments will not be large this year but will increase next year.

**Bryce: Is there any visibility on the next-generation Feynman architecture?**

Expert: It's still some way off—likely after the second half of 2028. No clear information yet.

**Bryce: For future upgrades, if power consumption continues to rise, will diamond or other materials be used? Is liquid metal just a transitional material for 2–3 years before being replaced?**

Expert: Yes, materials like graphene and diamond may be used in the future. Diamond is essentially a structure and may be applied in different forms, but it's still uncertain. Current investment enthusiasm is high, but there may be bubbles or oversupply in the future, and expectations for the next-generation Feynman architecture may not be as high.

**Bryce: You mentioned diamond as a supporting material, but it wasn't included in the 5% supporting value estimate. How should this be understood?**

Expert: Diamond is an additional increment. For TIM materials, GB300 has one layer, Rubin has two. Diamond is mainly used for training chips, with another supporting layer beneath the diamond. In inference servers, TIM1 is typically between the cold plate and chip lid, and TIM2 is between the chip and package lid. Different server configurations use different solutions. It depends on requirements—this part can be swapped out with the tray; for inference, a standard tray suffices.

The market is still dominated by inference, with training accounting for about 30% and the remaining 70% mainly inference. Many companies only purchase inference servers and do not need training servers. If a company wants to offer such services without heavy investment or high energy consumption, it can just buy some inference servers.

**Bryce: What is the industry competitive landscape? Who are the main competitors?**

Expert: The market is still dominated by international suppliers, who are more mature in materials. Internationally, the main players are Indium Corporation and Boyd; domestically, there are several, including Taijino, our company, Sinopec, and Fironda.

**Bryce: What is the domestic suppliers' market share in GB300 and future Rubin?**

Expert: Leading overseas suppliers hold about 70%; no single domestic supplier exceeds 10%.

Bryce: Does this refer to liquid metal only, or does it include diamond?

Expert: We do not supply diamond at present—no shipments yet. We may source from domestic synthetic diamond suppliers in the future, but it's still in R&D. Our company mainly produces liquid metal and specialty thermal grease, as well as standard thermal grease.

Bryce: Is specialty thermal grease a substitute for liquid metal? GB300 used thermal grease before, but not anymore, correct?

Expert: That's the case for NVIDIA, but in other sectors like aerospace, rail transit, and mobile phones, some customers still use modified thermal grease.

Bryce: For NVIDIA products, can the incremental revenue be directly calculated as the RMB 300 or 500 increase, or is it partly offset by the replacement of thermal grease?

Expert: It should be considered incremental. Most servers we supply to Foxconn still use thermal grease, but the volume is not large and has limited impact on the overall server market. While some market share is replaced, the overall effect is minor.

Bryce: I mean, for NVIDIA's GB300 and Rubin, assuming per-chip power remains unchanged, if thermal grease used to cost about RMB 17–18 per GPU, and liquid metal is now RMB 300, what is the incremental value?

Expert: Previously, thermal grease per GPU was about RMB 17–18, which is already high due to our improvements; standard products may cost just a few RMB.

**Bryce: What are the main barriers in the liquid metal field?**

Expert: The main barriers are customer relationships and technology. Customer recognition is crucial; technically, it's about material formulation and process. While anyone can research the formula, the specific ratios and processes are key. We've worked with Foxconn for years, making it easier to enter NVIDIA's testing and whitelist. It's difficult for new suppliers to gain Foxconn's trust.

Bryce: Given your long history with Foxconn Industrial Internet, why is your market share still in single digits?

Expert: Mainly because NVIDIA initially trusted overseas suppliers more.

Bryce: Now that you've passed validation, will your Rubin share increase?

Expert: Rubin share will be higher, but overall growth is limited due to capacity constraints—customers won't give all orders to one supplier, and industry relationships are complex. Overall, the liquid metal and supporting materials market is still dominated by international suppliers, with limited domestic share. Future material upgrades may move toward graphene, diamond, and other new materials, but liquid metal will remain mainstream in the short term. Industry barriers are mainly customer relationships and process technology, with market share limited by customer trust and capacity allocation.

**Bryce: Will your prices be 20% lower than overseas suppliers?**

Expert: The industry is aware, but there's a process of "demystification." Previously, we thought foreign products were better, and they do have advantages in quality control and technical history.

Bryce: For liquid cooling systems, are there concerns about issues, especially those directly related to chips, where a single chip could cause problems?

Expert: Absolutely. If a change is made, those responsible are often reluctant to take on the risk. If nothing goes wrong, it's fine, but if there is a problem, the responsibility is significant.

**Bryce: In terms of failure rates, is there a big gap between overseas and Chinese mainland suppliers?**

Expert: Actually, there's another issue—for example, Boyd's products are also manufactured in China. Danfoss's quick connectors are also made domestically. In essence, they're also seeking cost reduction. Overseas suppliers have higher costs because, after manufacturing in China, they have to export or do further processing locally before selling, which raises prices. NVIDIA is aware of this and is now bringing in more suppliers for direct cooperation. Previously, integrators handled negotiations, but now NVIDIA is more proactive. Although overseas prices are higher, the structure is relatively fixed for various reasons. Now, more effort is being put into developing Chinese mainland suppliers, which is ongoing.

If Boyd's volume is small, they can supply themselves. Once volume increases, it's hard to expand capacity or have workers work overtime. They can authorize other domestic companies, so there are no real technical barriers. Overseas suppliers have historical advantages, but domestic companies can match them through increased R&D and efficiency.

**Bryce: In the future, will supplier share be more determined by relationships with server integrators rather than NVIDIA itself?**

Expert: Correct. Currently, most dealings are with integrators, with limited direct contact with NVIDIA. Unless you have a very close relationship with NVIDIA and they help push your selection, integrators remain the main channel.

**Bryce: Will cold plate contract manufacturing affect the growth of metal material share? Can this business help?**

Expert: Yes. Bundling cold plates, liquid metal, interface materials, and rubber seals can impact material share, though not cover 100%. This approach offers customers more options—they only need to work with one supplier. So, it has a positive effect.

**Bryce: How is your relationship with companies like Tianhong and Wistron?**

Expert: No cooperation with Tianhong; relations with Wistron are good, and we've supplied them before. This refers to Wistron, not Wistron NeWeb.

**Bryce: What is the gross margin range for these materials in the industry—20% to 30%?**

Expert: Gross margins are generally 40%–60%, which is quite high. It depends on process control—for example, how much finished product you get from a ton of raw material. With good technology and high yield, margins are even higher, but at least 40%.

**Bryce: Regarding future trends, besides diamond and graphene, what other trends do you see—such as microfluidics or on-chip cooling?**

Expert: Everyone is looking for ways to improve heat transfer efficiency. We've previously developed graphene powder additives for liquid metal, which have improved performance and are still being tested. On-chip cooling requires collaboration with packaging houses, which is still challenging and progressing slowly. Building relationships with TSMC and NVIDIA is a slow process; for now, most improvements are in the materials themselves.

**Bryce: Of these trends, which do you see as most promising?**

Expert: Microfluidics may be the most promising. Our current channel designs for GB200 and GB300 have very fine channels and much improved machining precision. Future advances will come in flow rate, pressure, and two-phase transitions. If the price of fluorinated liquids drops, immersion cooling could also become mainstream. For now, fluorinated liquids are still expensive.

**Bryce: If future technology directions change, which trends would benefit your company, and which would not?**

Expert: Technologies focused on heat transfer and conduction are beneficial. If immersion cooling is adopted, it would have little to do with us. Immersion cooling is costly, requires frequent maintenance, and constant fluid filtration and testing. With our technology, equipment can run for five years without intervention.
